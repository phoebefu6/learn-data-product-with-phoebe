Infrastructure - the plumbing everything else stands on

Infrastructure is the boring-on-purpose tier: nobody demos it in a board meeting, yet every dashboard, model, and AI agent in the next four sessions stands on it. This session walks the seven products that decide where Kasa's data lands and where it lives - the cloud it runs on, the monitoring that watches it, the schema and dictionary that give it shape and meaning, and the dump-to-lake-to-warehouse flow that turns raw exports into a trusted, query-fast source. Get this rung right and everything above it is possible. Get it wrong and you are building on sand.

Part 0

The tier nobody applauds, and everything needs

When Kasa's leadership says "we want AI", they are pointing at the top of the ladder. But you cannot serve an answer you cannot store, trust a number you cannot trace, or join two tables that were never designed to meet. Infrastructure is the tier that makes the rest legal. It answers one blunt question - "where does our data live, and how do we get to it reliably?" - through seven products that split into two jobs: where data lands (the cloud, the monitoring, the schema, the dictionary) and where data lives (the raw dump, the flexible lake, the modeled warehouse). None of these are glamorous. All of them are load-bearing. This session is your commissioning checklist for the base of the ladder.

Part 1 · where data lands

The ground, the watchtower, and the blueprint 18 min live

Before data can live anywhere useful, it has to land somewhere reliable and arrive with shape and meaning. Four products do this. Cloud Infra is the ground you build on. APM is the watchtower that tells you when a pipeline breaks. Schema Design is the blueprint that decides how tables fit together. Data Dictionary is the plain-language catalog that stops the "whose number is right?" fight. Skip any one and the tier above inherits the mess.

LiveCloud Infra - the ground you rent instead of run4 min▶
What it isManaged compute, storage, and network you rent from a provider instead of buying and running your own servers.
Kasa exampleRaj lifts the 180 on-prem store databases into the cloud so POS data is no longer trapped on a box in each store's back office.
Business valueElastic cost that scales with load, no upfront capex, and far faster to stand up new capacity - build in days, not procurement quarters.
Reach for it whenYou are scaling past a single server, your load is spiky, or your data is trapped in places it cannot be reached.
Real world

Kasa's data was not missing - it was marooned. Each store's sales sat on a local machine, reachable only by someone physically there or a nightly email. Lifting those 180 databases to the cloud did not add a single new number, but for the first time all 180 stores were reachable from one place at the same time. That single move is what makes every product in this course even possible - you cannot serve data you cannot reach.

LiveApp Performance Monitoring (APM) - the watchtower4 min▶
What it isTooling that watches the latency, errors, and traces of running apps and data pipelines, and alerts you when something breaks or slows.
Kasa exampleThe nightly ETL silently half-loads one region's POS data. APM catches the row-count drop and pages Raj before Monday's numbers go out wrong.
Business valueYou find breakage before the business does - no more "the dashboard was wrong for a week and nobody noticed".
Reach for it whenAny pipeline or app feeds a decision - the moment silence could mean "broken" rather than "fine".
Real world

The scariest data failures are the quiet ones. Kasa's nightly load once completed "successfully" while dropping half of Malaysia's transactions - the job did not error, it just loaded less. For two weeks the MY revenue number looked merely soft, and three teams argued about consumer sentiment. APM would have flagged the row-count anomaly on night one. The lesson: a pipeline that does not error is not the same as a pipeline that worked.

LiveData Schema Design - the blueprint4 min▶
What it isThe deliberate shape of your tables, keys, and data types - decided before data lands, not patched afterward.
Kasa exampleRaj models orders, customers, and payments once, cleanly, so a customer ID means the same thing in the app, the POS, and Kasa Pay.
Business valueJoins that actually work, no expensive rework, and new data sources that slot in instead of fighting the existing shape.
Reach for it whenYou are standing up any new store, system, or data source - design the shape before the first row arrives.
Real world

Kasa's original systems each grew their own idea of a "customer" - the app used an email, the POS used a phone number, Kasa Pay used a wallet ID. Nothing joined. Every cross-channel question needed a data scientist to hand-stitch identities for a week. Designing one clean schema up front - shared keys, agreed types - is unglamorous work that pays back on every single join for years. Schema is the cheapest thing to get right early and the most expensive to fix late.

LiveData Dictionary - the shared meaning4 min▶
What it isA plain-language catalog of every field: what it means, how it is calculated, and who owns it.
Kasa exampleOne agreed definition of "active customer" - is it 30-day, 90-day, purchase or login? - written down, owned, and linked to the actual column.
Business valueKills the "whose number is right?" fight. Meetings stop being about definitions and start being about decisions.
Reach for it whenMore than one team reads the same data - the instant a term can mean two different things to two people.
Real world

Kasa's three "revenue" numbers were not a data problem - they were a meaning problem. Finance netted returns, marketing counted gross, store ops used shipped value. All three were "correct" and all three were different. A data dictionary does not compute anything new; it just writes the definition down once, names an owner, and ends the argument. It is the cheapest governance product on the ladder and often the highest trust-per-dollar.

Part 2 · where data lives

From raw drop to trusted source 14 min live

Once data lands on solid ground with shape and meaning, it needs somewhere to live. Three products form a pipeline that gets progressively more refined. The Data Dump is the raw catch - lose nothing. The Data Lake holds everything of any shape, cheaply, and decides structure later. The Data Warehouse is the modeled, clean, query-fast source that analytics actually runs on. The relationship between the last two is the single most-confused idea in data infrastructure, so it gets the flagship diagram.

LiveData Dump - lose nothing3 min▶
What it isA raw, as-is export of source data, captured without transforming or interpreting it.
Kasa exampleA nightly raw drop of the full POS system - every field, exactly as the till recorded it, no cleaning.
Business valueCheap capture with nothing lost - if you did not model a field today, you can still recover it tomorrow because the raw is kept.
Reach for it whenYou must land data before you fully understand it - capture first, make sense of it later.
Why "keep the raw" is a discipline, not laziness The instinct is to clean data on the way in. But every cleaning step is a decision, and decisions have bugs. Keeping the raw dump means when a downstream number looks wrong, you can always go back to what the source actually said - the ultimate ground truth.
LiveData Lake - keep everything, decide later4 min▶
What it isA cheap store for raw and semi-structured data of any shape - logs, clickstream, images, tables - all in one place.
Kasa exampleApp logs, web clickstream, POS exports, and Kasa Pay wallet events all land in one lake, each in its own zone.
Business valueKeep everything and decide the schema at read time (schema-on-read) - you never throw away data just because you have no use for it yet.
Reach for it whenThe variety and volume of your data outgrows what a warehouse alone can hold cleanly.
Real world

Kasa's data scientists could not build a fraud model in Session 4 without wallet event logs - but nobody had "designed a table" for those logs two years earlier. Because the lake had been quietly catching them raw the whole time, the history was there when it was finally needed. That is the lake's superpower: it lets you keep options open cheaply. The cost is discipline - a lake without governance quietly becomes a swamp nobody trusts.

LiveData Warehouse - the one trusted, fast source4 min▶
What it isA modeled, query-fast store of clean, conformed data built specifically for analytics and reporting.
Kasa exampleThe conformed sales and customer marts that Sofia's BI dashboards and the CFO scorecard all run on - one shape, one truth.
Business valueOne trusted, fast source for reporting - fast because it is modeled ahead of time (schema-on-write), trusted because it is conformed.
Reach for it whenYou need reliable, repeatable analytics that many people rely on - the moment reporting has to be dependable.
Real world

The warehouse is where Kasa's "one revenue number" finally becomes real. Raw POS lands in the dump, everything of any shape pools in the lake, and then the warehouse takes the pieces that matter and models them into clean, conformed sales marts - defined once, matching the data dictionary, fast to query. When the CFO asks "how did we do last week?", the warehouse is what answers in seconds with a number every team agrees on. Everything in Session 3 stands on it.

Lake and warehouse are not rivals - they are a relay A common mistake is treating "lake vs warehouse" as a either/or purchase. In a mature setup they work together: the lake keeps everything cheaply and flexibly, the warehouse serves the modeled subset that analytics needs fast and trusted. The pipeline below shows how the raw dump feeds both.
Flagship · the one to get right

Data lake vs data warehouse, side by side ★ 8 min · everyone

If you remember one distinction from this session, make it this one. A lake takes data raw, in any shape, and figures out structure only when you read it. A warehouse insists on clean, modeled structure before data goes in. Same raw dump feeds both - one optimized for keep-everything flexibility, the other for trusted-and-fast answers.

The plain-English test Ask: "do I know exactly how this data will be used?" If yes, model it into the warehouse. If not yet, land it in the lake and decide later. Most mature orgs run both, with the lake feeding a curated slice into the warehouse.
Before Session 3

This week ◐ 20 min total

  • Find your marooned data. Name one dataset in your org that is trapped somewhere it cannot be reached - a local machine, one person's laptop, a system nobody can query. That is a Cloud Infra job waiting to happen.
  • Test your monitoring. If your most important pipeline silently loaded half its data tonight, who would find out, and how long would it take? If the answer is "the business, eventually", you have an APM gap.
  • Count your definitions. Pick one term everyone uses - "active customer", "revenue", "churn" - and ask three teams what it means. Write down the differences. That gap is your data dictionary's first entry.
  • Optional: for one data source, decide out loud whether it belongs in a lake or a warehouse, using the one-line test. Notice how the decision hinges on "do I know how it will be used yet?"
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the core difference between a data lake and a data warehouse?

The distinction is about when structure is applied. A lake keeps everything raw and decides structure at read time; a warehouse enforces a clean, modeled shape before data loads, which is what makes it trusted and fast.

2 · Kasa's nightly ETL silently half-loaded a region's data for two weeks. Which product is designed to catch that?

APM watches pipelines for latency, errors, and anomalies like a sudden row-count drop. A job that completes without erroring is not the same as a job that worked - APM is what tells the difference.

3 · Kasa had three different "revenue" numbers across finance, marketing, and store ops. What fixes that fastest?

Three "revenue" numbers is a meaning problem, not a storage problem. All three were correct under different definitions. A data dictionary writes the definition down once, names an owner, and ends the argument - the cheapest trust-per-dollar on the ladder.
